MedGap — predicting psychiatric medication gaps before they become crises.

A clinical decision-support tool for prescribers to identify, triage, and act on psychiatric medication discontinuation across a patient panel. Two XGBoost models on ~740,000 All of Us records, SHAP-based clinician explanations, equity breakdowns, and auto-drafted patient outreach.

★ 1st place · FAU All of Us Hackathon 2026 dataNIH All of Us stackXGBoost · React · SHAP repogithub.com/shans15/medgap

Problem

Psychiatric medications are among the highest-stakes prescriptions in primary care and psychiatry. Discontinuation rates are notoriously high — in many cohorts more than half of patients stop refilling within a year — and unplanned discontinuation is a leading proximal cause of behavioral-health crisis events.

Prescribers operate without panel-level visibility. The signals exist in EHR data (refill cadence, prior crisis events, social-determinant flags, prescription history) but no one is paid to assemble them into a per-patient risk view at the moment a clinician needs to make an outreach decision. The patients most likely to fall off the regimen are exactly the ones whose follow-up appointments are easiest to miss.

The question MedGap answers: for each patient on this panel, what is the probability they will stop refilling in the next 45 days, and the probability they will have a behavioral-health crisis in the next 180 days — and what should the prescriber do about it?

Constraints

  • Data lives in the NIH All of Us Researcher Workbench. ~740,000 medication eras across many patients. The Workbench has aggressive egress controls — no patient-level data leaves the environment. The dashboard reads model outputs through a sanctioned channel, not the raw cohort.
  • Hackathon timebox. 48-hour window. No room for unbounded feature engineering or 12-model architecture searches. The work had to commit to a model family early and spend the remaining time on labels, calibration, and the clinician-facing layer.
  • Clinical trust is the bottleneck, not accuracy. A 0.92 AUC model nobody acts on is worth less than a 0.85 model with an explanation panel a prescriber understands inside thirty seconds.
  • Equity is a first-class result, not an afterthought. The All of Us cohort is intentionally diverse; the model has to perform across demographic groups, and the dashboard has to surface where it doesn't.

Approach

The whole system is a textbook Signal → Rank → Explain pipeline applied to a clinical panel.

01 / Signal — what survives the upstream filter

The cohort isn't the full Workbench population. It's narrowed to patients with at least one psychiatric medication era within the window and the minimum encounter history to make a 180-day-ahead prediction credible. The signal stage is intentionally cheap: deterministic filters, no model calls, no embedding lookups. The expensive work waits.

02 / Rank — two XGBoost models, one per question

Two models, not one. Splitting them lets each optimize for the right horizon and label.

  • Model 2 — Discontinuation Risk. 45-day window. Label derived from refill cadence. Higher recall on near-term drop-off is the goal — this is the model that drives outreach.
  • Model 1 — Crisis Risk. 180-day window. Label derived from downstream crisis-event codes. Lower recall is acceptable; the model is a triage filter on top of clinical judgment, not a replacement for it.

Both models are XGBoost. The choice is deliberate: in tabular EHR-style data with mixed categorical/continuous features and high feature interaction, gradient-boosted trees consistently beat or tie deep architectures while training in minutes on the Workbench compute budget. Equally important, the family supports SHAP attributions out of the box — which the next stage depends on.

03 / Explain — what the prescriber actually sees

The dashboard renders three things per patient: a thresholded risk band (High / Medium / Low), a SHAP waterfall showing which features pushed the prediction (current refill gap, recent crisis flag, panel-stability score, etc.), and a drafted outreach message the prescriber can edit and send.

Equity is rendered at the panel level, not buried in an appendix. The dashboard surfaces AUC and predicted-positive rates broken down by self-reported race, ethnicity, and sex, with a visible warning when subgroup performance falls below a configurable threshold. A clinician should never be told "the model says X" without also being told "the model is less reliable on patients like this."

The PR-AUC lifts are the more interesting numbers. In a high-class-imbalance regime — most patients don't discontinue in any given 45-day window — AUC-ROC overstates performance. PR-AUC is honest. 4.3× baseline on discontinuation and 3.7× baseline on crisis are the lifts that justify acting on the model output rather than blanket outreach.

Outcome: Won first place at the FAU All of Us Hackathon 2026 out of ~30 entering teams. Judging weighted clinical applicability, equity treatment, and demonstration quality alongside raw model performance.

What I'd change

This is the section I most want a hiring manager or research collaborator to read. Hackathon timeboxes leave honest gaps. The model is good. The pipeline around it has obvious next iterations.

  • Calibration before thresholds. The risk bands (High / Med / Low) currently use raw probability cutoffs at 0.35 and 0.65. Those thresholds should be set on a calibrated probability — Platt-scaled or isotonic-regressed against held-out positives — not raw XGBoost output. Without calibration the bands aren't directly comparable across cohorts.
  • Drift monitoring on the equity panel, not just on overall AUC. The dashboard currently shows subgroup AUC as a static one-time evaluation. A production version would re-evaluate on rolling cohorts and alert when subgroup performance degrades, even if global AUC stays flat. Where the model fails first is rarely on the average patient.
  • Outreach-as-a-treatment, evaluated. Right now the dashboard drafts an outreach message; clinicians act on it; nothing closes the loop. The right v2 is a quasi-experimental analysis that estimates whether MedGap-prompted outreach actually changes refill behavior. Lift on the model is necessary but not sufficient — lift on the outcome is the real product question.
  • The 180-day crisis window is too long to act on at panel scale. A clinician can act on a 45-day discontinuation prediction (call the patient, schedule a follow-up). A 180-day crisis prediction is mostly a research output. v2 should add a near-term (30-day) crisis-precursor model that gives the clinician something to do this week.
